I am a second-year PhD candidate in the [Cavendish Radio Astronomy and Cosmology](https://www.cavendishradiocosmology.com) Group at the [Kavli Institute for Cosmology](https://www.kicc.cam.ac.uk), University of Cambridge, funded by the [Harding Distinguished Scholars Programme](https://www.hardingscholars.fund.cam.ac.uk). My research combines the latest statistical and probabilistic machine learning methods with modern hardware accelerators and distributed computing to solve previously intractable inference problems in cosmology.

My work is centred on unveiling the first billion years of cosmic history, a period for which observations remain sparse and indirect. When did the first stars form, and what were they like? How did the first galaxies and black holes heat and ionise the hydrogen around them? And how did the Universe go from dark and neutral to the ionised, galaxy-filled cosmos we see today?

The 21-cm hyperfine transition of neutral hydrogen offers a direct window onto this era; however, detecting it is a formidable inference challenge. The signal lies buried beneath foregrounds many orders of magnitude brighter and is distorted by the complex electromagnetic response of the antenna and receiver chain, making its detection acutely sensitive to model misspecification. Tackling this requires both an accurate generative model and an efficient way to invert it. I develop physically motivated forward models, spanning astrophysical simulations to computational electromagnetics, and design inference methods that solve the resulting inverse problems with rigorous Bayesian uncertainty quantification. This has also led me to co-develop open-source tools such as [JAXMg](https://github.com/flatironinstitute/jaxmg), a distributed multi-GPU linear algebra library for JAX. I work primarily on global 21-cm cosmology with [REACH](https://www.reachtelescope.org), and on frameworks for exascale data from next-generation interferometers such as the [Square Kilometre Array Observatory](https://www.skao.int/en). Ultimately, I aim to extract the maximal scientific information from astronomical datasets, with methods that scale with data volume, model complexity and dimensionality, and extend across astrophysics, from broader cosmology to gravitational-wave astronomy.

Before my PhD, I completed the [MPhil in Data Intensive Science](https://mphildis.bigdata.cam.ac.uk), jointly taught by the Department of Applied Mathematics and Theoretical Physics, the Department of Physics and the Institute of Astronomy. My work focused on developing unsupervised machine learning frameworks to study the assembly history of the Milky Way, alongside projects ranging from gravitational-wave cosmology to LoRA fine-tuning of transformers. I hold a BSc in Physics from Durham University, where I carried out research within the [Centre for Extragalactic Astronomy](https://www.astro.dur.ac.uk/CEA/).

Outside academia, I am passionate about endurance sports. I rowed competitively as part of Durham University’s British Rowing Programme and in 2024 completed the [Great Norse Run](https://race-nation.co.uk/register/rock2recovery/great-norse-run-2024): nine marathons in ten days across Iceland, raising over £21,000 for emergency-services mental health.
